About

What interests me most about finance is the gap between what a company reports and what the market believes.

My work brings together accounting, valuation, and empirical research. I build three-statement financial models, DCFs, and LBOs, and use Python alongside CRSP, Bloomberg, and WRDS data to test investment ideas. I'm particularly interested in what financial statements reveal about a business, how risk translates into valuation, and whether market prices reflect the underlying economics.

My current research examines whether ESG ratings provide meaningful information to investors in the oil and gas industry, particularly when different rating agencies reach conflicting conclusions about the same company.

I came to finance from civil engineering, where I learned that even the most sophisticated model is only as reliable as its assumptions. That principle continues to shape how I approach financial analysis: question the assumptions, examine the evidence, and let the numbers support the conclusion.

  • Accounting information and equity returnsHow reported fundamentals capture risk and predict returns, and why some accounting-based anomalies persist
  • Disclosure and ESG ratingsWhat firms disclose, how rating agencies turn it into scores, and why those scores disagree
  • International differences in reportingHow disclosure expectations differ across markets for firms that trade on the same exchange

Research

Does ESG still pay in oil & gas? In progress

Tests whether ESG scores, and each E, S and G pillar on its own, explain shareholder returns for 34 US-listed oil and gas firms from 2017 to 2026, and whether the relationship changed after the 2022 energy shock. The practical question is whether an investor using ESG data would have beaten the sector benchmark.

Methods: panel of returns against pillar scores, an event study of five ESG strategy announcements by Shell, BP and Exxon, rating disagreement across Bloomberg, MSCI and Sustainalytics, and ESG-linked DCF scenarios. Data: Bloomberg, WRDS.

Which ESG dimension drives downside risk? Evidence from US tech firms

Team project · MGTF 415

Using about 400 US technology firms from 2015 to 2024, we regress downside volatility (the volatility of each stock's losing months) on total ESG, on the E, S and G pillars together, and on their first principal component. Higher ESG scores go with lower downside risk, and governance shows the strongest link.

Data: CRSP via WRDS, Bloomberg ESG scores.

Performance and evaluation of an eco-friendly composite thermal insulation material from agricultural waste

Undergraduate thesis, NMIT · Best Project Award

We made low-density particle boards from bamboo and sugarcane bagasse and tested three layer configurations against Indian Standards. Layering bagasse with bamboo roughly halved water absorption (about 24% against 45%) and cut thickness swelling from 21% to 6%.
